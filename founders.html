What we find

Four patterns show up often, across practices of many sizes. None of them are failures. They are what can happen when years go into serving clients, which is what good advisors do, rather than into building a salable asset.

In many practices, clients are loyal to the founder rather than to the firm.

This is often one of the largest drivers of price, and the slowest to change. Relationships can begin to shift after about three consecutive meetings where someone other than the founder leads the agenda. At two meetings a year, that can mean an eighteen-month project per household, run in waves. Starting it after a letter of intent is signed leaves little time to finish it.

What you take home is not what a buyer will pay a multiple on.

A buyer restates owner compensation to what it would cost to hire someone to do your job, then applies the multiple to what is left. Many founders first see that number in a diligence memo, which is a costly time to find out.

Several advisors sharing an office does not always add up to one firm.

If everyone has their own book and their own economics, a buyer sees several separate deals and prices each one alone. Processes like this can stall when the partners have not agreed on what they are selling.

The buy-sell agreement already decides much of this, whether or not anyone has reread it.

How price is set, whether discounts apply, what is funded and for which triggers, and who can veto a buyer. Many partners have not reread theirs since signing, even as the firm has grown well beyond what it covered.

What waiting costs

There is no dramatic moment where value disappears. Clients age into drawdown. Your remaining working years fall below the time it takes to move a relationship. Growth flattens as prospecting stops being the priority. None of it feels like anything while it is happening.

The effect often becomes visible later, in a letter of intent, as a longer earnout and less cash at close. Key-person concentration is how much of the value depends on one advisor. In a practice with high concentration and a founder past sixty-five, enterprise value can erode by an estimated six to ten percent a year while this goes unaddressed.

The reverse also holds. The same profit, inside a firm that has done this work, often prices higher, not because you won new clients but because what you are selling has changed.

The question we ask first We start with what the day after closing looks like, not with what you want for the practice in the abstract. Founders who have not pictured that day in detail are more likely to renegotiate at the letter of intent, and it is better to work through that in year one than in year three.
The question we ask last We end by asking what happens to your family if you are unable to work before any of this is settled. For many founders, that question changes how urgent the rest feels.
