For founders

Succession rarely comes with a deadline, which is one reason the work often starts late.

In many practices, a health event or an approach from a buyer is what first raises the question. By then there is less room to choose the timing, structure and terms. This page covers four patterns to test against your own practice, what waiting may cost, and where the work starts.

Four patterns to test against your practice

These patterns come up often in practices of many sizes. None of them is a failure. They tend to follow from spending years serving clients rather than building an asset a buyer can value. Use them as questions to check against your own practice.

In many practices, clients are loyal to the founder rather than to the firm.

This is key-person concentration: the share of value that depends on one advisor. It is often one of the largest factors in price and one of the slowest to change. As an estimate, relationships can begin to shift after about three consecutive meetings in which someone other than the founder leads the agenda. At two meetings a year, that works out to about 18 months per household, run in waves. A transition started after a letter of intent is signed has little time to finish.

What you take home is not what a buyer will pay a multiple on.

A buyer restates owner compensation to what it would cost to hire someone to do the owner's job, then applies the multiple to the profit that remains. That figure is owner-adjusted profit. Founders may first see it in a buyer's diligence memo, and calculating it earlier leaves more time to act on it.

Several advisors sharing an office do not always add up to one firm.

If each advisor has a separate client book and separate economics, a buyer may see several deals and price each one alone. The process can stall if the partners have not agreed on what they are selling.

The buy-sell agreement often settles much of this before anyone negotiates.

It sets how price is determined, whether discounts apply, what is funded and for which triggers, and who can veto a buyer. If it was written when the firm was smaller, it may no longer match what the firm has become.

What waiting costs

Value rarely disappears at a single moment. It erodes through gradual changes. Clients age into drawdown, your remaining working years may fall below the time it takes to move a relationship, and growth can flatten when prospecting stops being a priority. Each change is slow, so it is easy to miss.

In a practice with high key-person concentration and a founder past 65, enterprise value may erode by an estimated 6% to 10% a year if the concentration goes unaddressed. The effect is often first visible in a buyer's offer, as a longer earnout (the part of the price paid later, depending on results) or less cash at close. Starting earlier gives you more choice over timing, structure and terms.

The reverse also holds. The same profit inside a firm that has done this work can price higher, because what you are selling has changed rather than because you added clients. The 6% to 10% figure is an estimate. Preparing earlier takes time and fees now, so the right trade-off depends on your practice, your timeline and the options you want to keep open.

The question we ask first We start with what the day after the transition looks like, not with what you want for the practice in the abstract. A concrete answer makes it easier to settle terms early instead of reopening them at the letter of intent.
The question we ask last We end by asking what would happen to your family if you were unable to work before any of this is settled. The answer helps decide how soon the rest should start.
