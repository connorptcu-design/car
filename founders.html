What we actually find

The same four things, in nearly every practice, whatever the size. None of them are failures. They are what happens when someone spends thirty years serving clients rather than building an asset, which is what good advisors do.

The clients belong to you, not to the firm.

It is the single largest driver of price and the slowest to change. Relationships move after roughly three consecutive meetings where someone else leads the agenda. At two meetings a year, that is an eighteen-month project per household, run in waves. Starting it after a letter of intent is signed is starting it two years late.

What you take home is not what a buyer will pay a multiple on.

A buyer restates owner compensation to what it would cost to hire someone to do your job, then applies the multiple to what is left. The first time most founders see that number is in a diligence memo, which is the most expensive moment to see it.

Several advisors sharing an office is not one firm.

If everyone has their own book and their own economics, a buyer sees several separate deals and prices each one alone. Most processes in this structure stall because the partners never agreed on what they were selling.

The agreement in the drawer has already decided most of this.

How price is set, whether discounts apply, what is funded and for which triggers, and who can veto a buyer. Most partners have not read theirs since signing, and it was written when the firm was a fraction of its current size.

What waiting costs

There is no dramatic moment where value disappears. Clients age into drawdown. Your remaining working years fall below the time it takes to move a relationship. Growth flattens as prospecting quietly stops being the priority. None of it feels like anything while it is happening.

It shows up all at once, in a letter of intent, as a longer earnout and less cash at close. For a practice with real key-person concentration and a founder past sixty-five, the erosion runs somewhere between six and ten percent of enterprise value a year.

The corollary is the reason to start. The same profit, inside a firm that has done this work, prices materially higher. Not because you won new clients. Because you changed what you were selling.

The question we ask first Not what you want for the practice. What the day after closing looks like. A founder who has never pictured that day is the one who renegotiates at the letter of intent, and it is better for everyone to find that out in year one than in year three.
The question we ask last What happens to your family if you are unable to work before any of this is settled. It reframes the entire conversation from optional to overdue, which is why it goes at the end.
