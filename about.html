About

A well-developed market exists for closing the sale. Less exists for the years that decide its price.

National succession firms and sell-side bankers are paid when a transaction closes. That fee structure means they tend to engage only once a founder has already decided to sell, and their pricing is built for practices large enough to carry a success fee. Less is built for advisors who are 2 to 10 years from that decision, especially those inside a wirehouse, a broker-dealer or an insurance career system, where the book may not be freely salable at all. For those advisors, support is often thinner for the years that set the price, and for finishing the transition on their own terms rather than a buyer's.

A market built for the close, not the decade before it

Many advisors spend something like thirty years building a practice and thirty days preparing to leave it, in part because little in this industry prompts them to do otherwise.

40%of industry assets are held by advisors who expect to retire within ten years
1 in 4of those advisors are still unsure how they will exit
3rdwhere independent advisors rank valuing their practice among their succession challenges, behind finding a qualified buyer and structuring deal terms

Sources: Cerulli Associates, U.S. Advisor Metrics 2024; The Cerulli Edge, U.S. Advisor Edition, 3Q 2026.

Firms compensated on a success fee have no structural way to advise a founder to wait, even when waiting is the better choice. Firms organized around a minimum deal size tend to price practices below that line in similar terms. Because many engage only once a letter of intent is already on the table, that is often the point at which a founder first sees the economics in detail.

The timeline is not generous

A meaningful share of the advisors managing this industry's assets expect to exit inside a similar window. The number of well-capitalized buyers is unlikely to grow at the same pace. Acquirers tend to be selective about size, growth and model, and sellers who are prepared typically get first look; those who are not have fewer options.

The work that moves the number most — relationship transition, restated economics, an agreement that matches what the firm has become — can take an estimated three years or more to show up. Starting it only after deciding to sell leaves less time for that work to take effect, and fewer options for how the transition is structured.

The cost of waiting Key-person concentration is how much of the value depends on one advisor. In a practice with high concentration and a founder past sixty-five, enterprise value can erode by an estimated six to ten percent a year while this goes unaddressed. The effect is often first visible in a buyer's offer, as a longer earnout or less cash at close. Starting earlier gives you more choice over timing, structure and terms.

What closing the gap requires

It requires arithmetic, sequencing, and asking the questions in an order that makes them answerable. The questions worth answering before a buyer asks them

Every one of these gets asked eventually. The only choice is whether you answer it now, on your terms, or in a diligence memo, on theirs.

Could you tell a buyer, today, who owns each client relationship — the firm, or you?

Key-person concentration is often one of the largest drivers of price and the slowest to fix. If the honest answer is "me," that is not a confession. It is the starting point.

Do you know what a buyer would pay after restating your compensation to a market salary?

Many founders see that number for the first time in a diligence memo, after the price has effectively already been set.

If every partner sold today, would a buyer be pricing one firm, or quietly pricing several?

Separate books and separate economics read as separate deals, no matter what the letterhead says.

What happens to your clients and your family if you cannot work tomorrow, with none of this decided?

It is the question most likely to change how urgent the rest feels.

If you hesitated on more than one of those, that is not a verdict. It is the first finding of the readiness diagnostic.

The difference is deliberate work Years spent building a practice do not automatically build something a buyer wants at a price that reflects what you built. That takes separate, deliberate preparation, started well before you expect to need it.
