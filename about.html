About

What a practice can be worth is shaped in the years before any transaction.

We work with advisors in those years, before a sale, a transfer or a step-back. That includes advisors inside a wirehouse, a broker-dealer or an insurance career system, where the book may not be freely salable. The work covers the factors that shape price and the choices available on timing, structure and terms. Our fees are a retainer for preparation and an hourly rate for transaction support, with no success fee.

3rdwhere independent advisors rank valuing their practice among their succession challenges, behind finding a qualified buyer and structuring deal terms

Sources: Cerulli Associates, 2026 (share of advisors retiring and assets, succession uncertainty); Cerulli Associates, January 2025 (seller challenges ranking).

Taken together, these figures describe a large group of advisors approaching a transition, one in four of them still unsure how they will exit. An advisor can spend something like 30 years building a practice and 30 days preparing to leave it, partly because the decision rarely comes with a deadline. Time is what lets an advisor choose among an internal sale, an outside sale, a platform, a phased step-back or continuing to practice. That is the case for starting preparation early.

Why timing matters

By the figures above, a large share of industry assets sits with advisors who expect to retire within ten years. The number of well-capitalized buyers may not grow at the same pace, and acquirers tend to be selective about size, growth and business model. Sellers who are prepared may have more choice than those who are not.

The work that tends to move the number most includes relationship transition, restated economics and an agreement that matches what the firm has become. We estimate it can take three years or more to show up in a buyer's offer. Starting only after a decision to sell leaves less time for that work to take effect, and fewer options for how the transition is structured.

What waiting can cost Key-person concentration is the share of a practice's value that depends on one advisor. In a practice with high key-person concentration and a founder past 65, enterprise value may erode by an estimated 6% to 10% a year if the concentration goes unaddressed. The effect is often first visible in a buyer's offer, as a longer earnout (the part of the price paid later, depending on results) or less cash at close. Starting earlier gives you more choice over timing, structure and terms.

What the work involves

The work is arithmetic and sequencing: asking the right questions in an order that lets you answer them. The questions worth answering before a buyer asks them

A buyer's diligence team is likely to ask each of these. Answering them yourself first gives you more time to act on what you find.

Could you tell a buyer today who owns each client relationship: the firm or you?

Key-person concentration is often one of the largest factors in price and one of the slowest to change. If the honest answer is "me," that is where the work starts.

Do you know what a buyer would pay after restating your compensation to a market salary?

Founders may first see that number in a buyer's diligence memo. Calculating it earlier leaves more time to act on it.

If every partner sold today, would a buyer price one firm or several separate client books?

Separate client books and separate economics can read to a buyer as separate deals, whatever the firm's name suggests.

What would happen to your clients and your family if you could not work tomorrow, with none of this decided?

The answer helps decide how soon the other questions need attention.

If you were unsure about more than one of these, that is not a verdict. It is the first finding of the readiness diagnostic.

Something good is never easy A practice worth passing on is built over years. Preparing it to pass on takes the same sustained effort, starting with an honest view of where the practice stands today.
