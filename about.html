About

There is a well-paid industry for closing the sale. There is almost nothing for the years that decide its price.

National succession firms and sell-side bankers are paid when a transaction closes, so they show up once a founder has already decided to sell, and they are priced for practices large enough to carry a success fee. Everyone else — the advisor two, five or ten years out, and especially the advisor inside a wirehouse, a broker-dealer or an insurance career system whose book may not be freely salable at all — is left with nothing built for the years that actually set the price, or for making sure the next chapter starts on their terms rather than a buyer's.

A market built for the close, not the decade before it

Most advisors spend thirty years building a practice and about thirty days preparing to leave it, because almost nothing in this industry asks them to do otherwise.

Sources: Cerulli Associates, U.S. Advisor Metrics 2024; The Cerulli Edge, U.S. Advisor Edition, 3Q 2026.

The firms that do this work well are real, and they are not built for most of this market. They are compensated on success, which means the advice to wait three years is one they are structurally unable to give you. They work in deal size, which means a practice below their minimum gets the same generic playbook as everyone else. And they arrive once a letter of intent is already on the table — the single most expensive moment to discover what you are actually selling.

The timeline is not generous

A meaningful share of the advisors managing this industry's assets will be looking for an exit inside the same ten-year window. The buyers will not multiply to match them. Well-capitalized acquirers are selective about size, growth and model, and the sellers who show up prepared get first look. The ones who do not get whatever is left.

The work that actually moves a number — relationship transition, restated economics, an agreement that matches what the firm has become — takes three years or more to show up. Starting it after you have decided to sell means starting it after the part of the timeline that mattered most has already closed.

The cost of waiting For a practice with real key-person concentration and a founder past sixty-five, enterprise value erodes somewhere between six and ten percent a year while this goes undone. None of it feels like anything while it is happening. It shows up all at once, in a longer earnout and less cash at close.

What closing the gap actually requires

Not wisdom. Arithmetic, sequencing, and a willingness to ask the questions in an order that makes them answerable. We take a retainer for preparation work and an hourly rate if you want us alongside you in a transaction — never a success fee, and never a referral fee from a buyer, a lender or a platform. That is what keeps the advice to wait three years on the table.

We build the models in front of you rather than hand you a number in a PDF, because a valuation you cannot interrogate is one you have to take on faith, and this is not a decision that should run on faith.

The questions worth answering before a buyer asks them

Every one of these gets asked eventually. The only choice is whether you answer it now, on your terms, or in a diligence memo, on theirs.

Could you tell a buyer, today, who actually owns each client relationship — the firm, or you?

Key-person concentration is the single largest driver of price and the slowest to fix. If the honest answer is "me," that is not a confession. It is the starting point.

Do you know what a buyer would pay after restating your compensation to a market salary?

Most founders see that number for the first time in a diligence memo, after the price has effectively already been set.

If every partner sold today, would a buyer be pricing one firm, or quietly pricing several?

Separate books and separate economics read as separate deals, no matter what the letterhead says.

What happens to your clients and your family if you cannot work tomorrow, with none of this decided?

It is the question that turns this from optional to overdue, which is why it goes last.

If you hesitated on more than one of those, that is not a verdict. It is the first finding of the readiness diagnostic.

Something good is never easy Thirty years builds a book. It does not automatically build something a buyer wants, at a price that reflects what you actually built. That takes separate, deliberate work, started well before you intend to need it — which is the only part of this that is genuinely hard, and the only part worth doing properly.
