If you have been offered equity, know which of four arrangements it is before you negotiate the percentage.

Four different things get called equity in advisory firms, and only two leave you owning anything. Here is how to tell them apart.

Work out the buy-in Book a review

Ask which of these it is, in these words

Real equity. Shares or units you buy and then own.

The only version you can sell. It is also the only one you have to fund, which is why firms often offer one of the others and use the same word.

Profits interest. A share of everything the firm becomes worth above today's value.

Often a good fit and underused relative to its benefits. It requires no cash up front, because the threshold is set at today's value, so you are paid only on what you help create afterward. It lives in an LLC and requires an 83(b) election filed within thirty days of the grant. Missing that window means the interest is taxed as ordinary income instead.

Phantom equity or appreciation rights. A future cash payment that tracks what equity would have been worth.

It can pay real money, but you own nothing, cannot sell it, and it usually ends if you leave. The firm can often amend the plan's terms unilaterally.

Revenue or profit share. A percentage of what the firm brings in.

Not equity. It can be reasonable as compensation, but it does not answer the ownership question.

What your staying is worth to them

A buyer prices a firm partly on whether clients stay once the founder leaves. If you are a reason clients would stay, the firm can be worth meaningfully more with you in it than without you. For a mid-sized practice, that gap can run into seven figures.

That number is rarely stated out loud, but it is central to your negotiating position. It helps to treat it as a shared problem rather than a threat: the firm is worth more once clients know you, and neither of you gets there without a written agreement and sustained joint meetings over time. You are asking the firm to let you build the relationships that create that value for both of you.

Consider your own counsel The firm's counsel represents the firm, which in practice often means the founder. They may be easy to work with, but they are not your lawyer. Independent counsel is a common safeguard, and it often costs a few thousand dollars against a decision of this size.
